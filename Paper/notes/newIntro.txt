In Boston, the average weekly rate of emergency department (ED) visits for influenza-like illness (ILI) was more than seven times higher in Dorchester (67 per 100,000 residents) than in Fenway (9 per 100,000) between 2022 and 2025 CITE. Neighborhoods within a single city differ in age structure, income, and housing, and these differences have long been tied to who becomes ill with influenza CITE. Yet, neighborhoods also sit side by side in a densely populated, highly connected city, where residents commute, work, and shop across neighborhoods and where influenza spreads between nearby places CITE.
Health departments act on this variation locally. Mobile vaccination clinics, school outreach, and clinician messaging are deployed neighborhood by neighborhood, often before the data needed to guide these decisions become available CITE. Forecasts can help inform these choices. Yet most influenza forecasting targets nations, regions, and states. Forecasting within a city is harder because each area has fewer cases and noisier weekly counts, and an earlier study in New York City found that linking areas improved borough forecasts but not neighborhood forecasts CITE.
Neighborhood influenza activity is plausibly shaped by several kinds of information at once: its own recent trajectory and seasonal timing, weather conditions that affect transmission, the demographic makeup of the neighborhood, and activity in neighboring areas. Many forecasting models use only one or two of these. Graph neural networks, which pass information along a map of connections, offer a way to combine all of them in a single model. Graph models for influenza have reported gains across regions, but the evidence is mixed: in forecasting hubs, many models failed to beat simple baselines in some seasons, autoregressive models remain competitive 1 to 4 weeks ahead, and in one dengue study a per-district recurrent network outperformed every graph variant. Reviews also caution that retrospective tests can overstate real-world accuracy.
It is therefore unclear whether integrating temporal, environmental, demographic, and geospatial information in a graph neural network improves neighborhood-level forecasts in a city like Boston, and where the gain comes from. We addressed this predictive question, not a causal one, by forecasting neighborhood ED visit rates for ILI 1, 2, and 4 weeks ahead in Boston’s 14 neighborhoods. We asked (1) whether the integrated model outperforms simple and machine learning baselines; (2) how much each component, environmental, demographic, and geospatial, adds to its forecasts; and (3) whether the map can be built from public boundary files and whether a model trained in one city can forecast another. To limit the usual ways a new model can look better than it is, we tested on seasons never used in design, gave every model the same tuning budget, and scored full forecast distributions. We repeated the analysis in Columbus, Ohio, and in Chicago, Illinois, a city never used while designing the model.
This study makes four contributions. First, it evaluates neighborhood-level influenza forecasts in a way designed to guard against overstated gains. Second, it separates what the environmental features, the demographic features, and the neighborhood map each add to a graph neural network. Third, it tests whether the map can be built automatically from public boundary files and whether a model trained in one city transfers to another, in Boston, Columbus, and Chicago.

\begin{center}
{\Large\bfseries Spatiotemporal Graph Neural Networks for Neighborhood-Level Influenza Forecasting in Boston\par}
\end{center}

\section*{Abstract}

Health departments act neighborhood by neighborhood, but most influenza
forecasts cover states or regions. We asked
whether a map of how neighborhoods connect improves neighborhood forecasts. We
forecast emergency department influenza-like illness 1, 2, and 4 weeks ahead in
Boston, Massachusetts (14 neighborhoods, 2022--2025); Columbus, Ohio
(2024--2025); and Chicago, Illinois (58 ZIP codes, 2022--2026). Seasons used to
design the model were excluded, and forecasts were scored with the weighted
interval score used by FluSight. In Boston, a graph neural network had 18\% to
28\% less error than assuming next week equals this week, and removing the map
increased its error in 8 of 9 season and horizon comparisons (clearly in 5). A
tuned recurrent neural network without a map matched it 1 week ahead; the
graph neural network's lead at 2 and 4 weeks was clear in 1 of 3 seasons. The
map could be built from public boundary files, and a Boston-trained model
forecast Columbus as well as a local model. In Chicago, never used in design,
error was 17\% to 19\% below persistence, and the map helped 1 week ahead in
all 4 seasons. The map's gain was modest but measurable.

\bigskip
\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} forecasting; graph neural networks; influenza, human;
machine learning; public health surveillance; small-area analysis

\bigskip
\noindent\textbf{Abbreviations:} ED, emergency department; ILI, influenza-like
illness; LSTM, long short-term memory network; WIS, weighted interval score.

\section*{Introduction}

Seasonal influenza affects up to 1 billion people worldwide each
year,\cite{yamauchi2025book} and in the United States its annual
economic burden has been estimated at \$11.2 billion.\cite{putri2018burden}
That burden is not shared evenly within a city. During the 1918 pandemic,
influenza mortality in Chicago differed from one census tract to the next and
tracked illiteracy and homeownership.\cite{grantz2016chicago} A century later,
influenza hospitalization rates in US census tracts where at least 20\% of
residents lived in poverty were nearly twice those in tracts where fewer than
5\% did.\cite{hadler2016poverty} In our data, from June 2022 to May 2025,
the average weekly rate of
emergency department (ED) visits for influenza-like illness (ILI) ranged from 9
per 100,000 residents in Boston's Fenway neighborhood to 67 per 100,000 in
Dorchester.

Health departments act on that variation locally. Mobile vaccination clinics,
school outreach, and messaging to clinicians are placed neighborhood by
neighborhood. Forecasts help when a decision has to be
made before the data that would settle it arrive,\cite{lipsitch2011evidence} and
public health agencies, including the Centers for Disease Control and
Prevention, have used influenza forecasts for risk communication and
planning.\cite{lutz2019applying} Most forecasting effort, however, targets
nations, regions, and states. The agency's FluSight challenge has compared forecasts of state and national
influenza activity for over a
decade,\cite{mcgowan2019collaborative,reich2019collaborative,mathis2024flusight}
and internet search methods estimate current activity rather than forecast
it\cite{ginsberg2009detecting,yang2015argo,lu2019statelevel} and can
drift.\cite{lazer2014parable}

Forecasting within a city is harder because each area has fewer cases and
noisier weekly counts. It is also where knowledge of neighboring areas might
help most: influenza spreads between nearby places\cite{gog2014pandemic}
and along commuting and contact networks.\cite{viboud2006synchrony,charu2017mobility,pei2018spatial}
Yang et al. forecast influenza in New York City boroughs and neighborhoods with
a network model and found that linking areas improved borough forecasts but not
neighborhood forecasts.\cite{yang2016nyc} Graph neural networks, machine learning
models that pass information along a map of connections, offer another way to
use this structure.\cite{kipf2017gcn,jin2023stgnn} Luo et al. reported that a
graph neural network with both geographic and correlation-based connections
improved influenza estimates across regions of Taiwan.\cite{luo2025novel}

The evidence that such models help in practice is mixed. In multimodel
forecasting hubs for influenza and COVID-19, individual models' skill varied
widely, and in some seasons only a minority beat a simple
baseline,\cite{mathis2024flusight,cramer2022covid} while plain autoregressive
models remain competitive 1 to 4 weeks ahead.\cite{kandula2019autoregressive}
In a study of dengue in Sri Lanka, a per-district recurrent neural network
outperformed every graph neural network variant that weighted neighboring
districts.\cite{gogovi2026spatial} Reviews note that the added value of
artificial intelligence for epidemics is hard to evaluate\cite{kraemer2025ai}
and that retrospective tests can overstate real-world
accuracy.\cite{kamyshnyi2026review}

We therefore asked a predictive question, not one about the causes of
influenza: when does a neighborhood map improve forecasts of neighborhood ED
visit rates for ILI in Boston 1 to 4 weeks ahead? The main comparison removes
the map and holds everything else fixed, within an evaluation built to avoid the usual ways a new model can look better than it
is: unseen test seasons, equal tuning for every model, and week-level tests on
full forecast distributions. We also asked whether the map can be built
automatically from public boundary files, and whether a model trained in one
city can forecast another. We repeated the analysis in Columbus, Ohio, and in
Chicago, Illinois, a city never used while designing the model, whose health
department reports a 1--10 activity level for each ZIP code.

\section*{Methods}

\subsection*{Setting and data}

Table~\ref{tab:data} summarizes the 3 cities. In Boston, the Boston Public
Health Commission publishes the weekly rate of ED visits for ILI per 100,000
residents for 14 neighborhoods;\cite{bphc2026dashboard} we used December 2017 through May 2026. ED visits track
laboratory-confirmed influenza closely, including by age
group.\cite{olson2007ed} Weeks with small counts are suppressed by the
commission; we treated them as missing rather than as zero. In Columbus,
Columbus Public Health provided visit-level ED records for ILI beginning in
January 2022, which we aggregated to the department's 17 planning areas through
a ZIP code crosswalk and divided by American Community Survey population.\cite{census2023acs} In Chicago,
the Department of Public Health publishes, from September 2019, a weekly ILI
activity level for each ZIP code from 1 to 10, comparing its share of ED visits
for ILI with its own summer average;\cite{cdph2026influenza} Chicago errors are therefore in levels
(Table 1; Appendix S1).

\subsection*{The neighborhood map}

We represented each city as a map in which every area is a point and 2 areas
are linked when they share a border (Figure 1A). Areas at the
edge of a city would otherwise look as if they had no neighbors on one side, so
we added surrounding areas that are part of the map but are never forecast or
scored: 7 in Boston (for example, Cambridge and Somerville, Brookline, and
Boston Harbor), 5 in Columbus, and 1 in Chicago (Appendix S1).

\medskip


Boston's map was first drawn by hand from a city map. To test whether a new
city could skip that step, we also built it automatically from public boundary
files,\cite{boston2026boundaries,census2023tigerweb,chicago2026zipboundaries} treating 2 areas as neighbors when their boundaries share an edge
rather than only a corner. Building neighbor lists from census boundary
files is standard in spatial disease mapping, as is adding links across water
by hand.\cite{morris2019bym} Links across the
Charles River and Boston Harbor were added by hand in every version. In addition to shared borders, which also reach 2 and 3 borders away
at decreasing weight, the model's default map links neighborhoods whose past
influenza curves were highly correlated (computed on weeks before the test
season only) and neighborhoods with similar demographics.

\subsection*{Forecasting model}

The graph neural network forecasts each neighborhood in 3 steps. First, it
reads the past 16 weeks of that neighborhood's ILI rate, together with weather
and demographics, and summarizes, from past weeks only, whether activity is rising or
falling. Second, it mixes each neighborhood's summary with those
of its neighbors on the map, a step known as graph
convolution.\cite{kipf2017gcn} The model can also learn additional links that
the map does not contain. Third, it adds a learned correction to a baseline
that blends 2 simple forecasts: the current week's rate and the usual rate for
that time of year, estimated from training seasons with a smooth seasonal
curve. The weight on each part of the blend is learned separately for each
forecast horizon (how many weeks ahead), so that 1-week forecasts can lean on the current week and 4-week
forecasts on the seasonal pattern (Appendix S1, Figure S1). A separate model was trained for each
horizon, and each forecast averages 10 models trained from
different random starting points.

Each model produced a full forecast distribution, reported as the 23 quantiles
(percentiles) FluSight uses.\cite{mathis2024flusight} All models except the FluSight baseline,
whose ranges come from past changes, got their ranges from one shared procedure
calibrated on validation weeks (the weeks just before each test season).

To measure what the map contributes, we trained an otherwise identical model
with the map removed (the no-map version): no links between neighborhoods,
mapped or learned, and no surrounding areas, so each neighborhood sees only its
own history.

\subsection*{Comparison models}

Appendix S1 details all comparison models, including repeating the same week
of the previous year. Persistence assumes next week will look like this week. The FluSight baseline carries the latest value forward
with ranges based on past changes over the forecast
horizon.\cite{mathis2024flusight,cramer2022covid} The
seasonal blend is the graph neural network's own baseline with the learned
correction switched off, so it involves no machine learning. We also fit a
seasonal autoregressive integrated moving average model for each neighborhood;
gradient-boosted regression trees (many small decision trees, each correcting
the errors of the last) on the same inputs as the graph neural network; a long
short-term memory network (LSTM), a recurrent neural network that reads each
series week by week and carries a memory forward, here reading all
neighborhoods' series together but with no map;\cite{hochreiter1997lstm}
and Cola-GNN, a published graph neural network for influenza that learns its
own connections between locations,\cite{deng2020colagnn} which we reimplemented
from its description. Two graph models from Luo et al.\cite{luo2025novel,velickovic2018gat}
were run at 1 week only and are reported in Appendix S1.

\subsection*{Evaluation design}

We used rolling-origin evaluation, which reproduces how a forecaster would
have performed in past seasons.\cite{tashman2000outofsample} For each test
season, every model was trained only on data before that season, tuned on the
weeks immediately before it, and then scored on its 52 target weeks (53 in Chicago 2025--2026).

The 2025--2026 season in Boston and Columbus was used while designing the
model, so we call it the development season and do not report it as evidence; its ablations are in Appendix S1, Table S16.
The remaining seasons were never used to choose anything: 2022--2023,
2023--2024, and 2024--2025 in Boston and 2024--2025 in Columbus. Chicago was
added after the design was fixed, so all 4 of its seasons, 2022--2023 through
2025--2026, are unseen (Figure 1B). Influenza nearly disappeared during the
first year of the COVID-19 pandemic,\cite{olsen2020decreased} so Columbus
models used weeks from July 2022 onward, leaving 1 unseen season; Boston and
Chicago models used their full histories.

A new model can also look better because more effort went
into tuning its settings. To prevent this, every machine learning model received the
same budget in every test season. We tried 12 combinations of settings (its
default plus 11 drawn at random from ranges of similar breadth for every model),
trained each once, kept the one with the lowest error on the validation weeks,
and retrained it 10 times (Appendix S1, Table S2). The same procedure was used
in all 3 cities (Appendix S1, Table S14).

\subsection*{Scoring}

Our main measure answers the question: How far off was the forecast, counting
both its best guess and how honest its range was? This is the weighted interval
score (WIS), the scoring rule used by FluSight and the COVID-19 Forecast
Hubs.\cite{bracher2021interval,mathis2024flusight} Each forecast gives a
median $m$ and $K = 11$ central intervals $[l_k, u_k]$ (98\% to 10\%); for
observed value $y$,
\begin{equation*}
\mathrm{WIS} = \frac{1}{K + \frac{1}{2}} \Bigl( \tfrac{1}{2}\lvert y - m\rvert
+ \sum_{k=1}^{K} \Bigl[ \tfrac{\alpha_k}{2}(u_k - l_k)
+ (l_k - y)^{+} + (y - u_k)^{+} \Bigr] \Bigr),
\end{equation*}
where $\alpha_k$ is 1 minus the interval's coverage and $(x)^{+} = \max(x, 0)$:
interval widths plus penalties for observations outside them (Appendix S1
explains the score and how to read a value, with a worked example). It is expressed in the units
of the forecast (ED visits per 100,000, or levels in Chicago), lower is better, and it
rewards ranges that are narrow but still contain what happened. Point measures
answer narrower questions: mean absolute error (how far off the best guess is,
on average), root mean squared error (the same, weighting large misses more),
and the correlation between forecast and observed rates in each neighborhood
(whether the forecast rises and falls at the right times). None scores the
range, so they are secondary. Relative WIS, a
model's mean WIS divided by that of persistence, is below 1 when the model
beats persistence. We checked calibration by asking how
often the observed value fell inside the 50\% and 95\% forecast
intervals.\cite{gneiting2007proper}

To compare 2 models within a season, we asked whether the gap between them
could be explained by which weeks the season happened to contain. We averaged
each model's WIS over neighborhoods for each week, estimated a 95\% confidence
interval for the difference by resampling blocks of 4 consecutive weeks (2,000
resamples),\cite{kunsch1989bootstrap} and tested it with the Diebold--Mariano
test,\cite{diebold1995comparing} using a small-sample
correction\cite{harvey1997testing} and a variance estimate that allows for the overlap between
consecutive multiweek forecasts.\cite{newey1987hac} Because each model was
compared against many others, 2-sided \textit{P} values were adjusted with the
Holm method\cite{holm1979simple} within each season and horizon. We call a
difference \textit{clear} when the adjusted \textit{P} value was below .05, and
not clear (a tie) otherwise.

\subsection*{Severity alerts}

We also asked whether a forecast would have warned that a neighborhood was
entering a moderate or high week. As in the Centers for Disease Control and
Prevention's severity classification,\cite{biggerstaff2018severity} we set
thresholds for each neighborhood with the Moving Epidemic
Method,\cite{vega2013mem} using only seasons before each test season and
leaving out those disrupted by COVID-19; moderate or higher means at or above the
50th-percentile intensity threshold, and high or higher at or above the 90th. We
scored 2 ways of alerting: from the forecast's probability of crossing the
threshold, with the Brier skill score\cite{brier1950verification} (above 0 is
better than always forecasting the average share of such weeks), and from
whether the best guess crossed it, as hit rate minus false-alarm rate (the
Peirce skill score). Differences got block-bootstrap intervals as above.
Chicago's measure is already a graded level, so it was not included.

\subsection*{Portability}

We ran 2 further tests with model settings fixed in advance. First, we
reran the Boston evaluation with 2 automatically built maps for the
graph neural network and for a borders-only version that learns no extra
links, so that the map is its only spatial information. Second, a version with no
neighborhood-specific learned links, which can run in any city, was trained on
one city's weeks before 2024--2025 and applied to another city's 2024--2025
season as is and after brief further training on local data (fine-tuning), and
compared with the same model trained from scratch there: from Boston to
Columbus and Chicago, and from each to Boston. We also trained it on 2 or 3
cities at once (Appendix S1).

Analyses used Python 3.12, PyTorch 2.14, PyTorch Geometric 2.8,
statsmodels 0.14, scikit-learn 1.9, and XGBoost 3.4; code and frozen settings
are released with the paper.\cite{code2026release}

\section*{Results}

\subsection*{Boston}

The graph neural network had the lowest
mean WIS at every horizon (Table~\ref{tab:boston}): 5.13 per 100,000 at 1 week,
6.47 at 2 weeks, and 8.22 at 4 weeks, against 6.24, 7.94, and 11.44 for
persistence. Its error was 18\% to 19\% lower than that of persistence at 1
and 2 weeks and 28\% lower at 4 weeks (Figure 2A). Its advantage
over persistence was clear in all 3 seasons at 1 week and in 2023--2024 at 2
and 4 weeks. The
graph neural network also had the lowest mean absolute error and root mean
squared error at every horizon. By correlation, the tuned LSTM and Cola-GNN were
slightly higher at 1 week (Appendix S1, Table S9).

\subsection*{What the neighborhood map adds}

Removing the map increased the model's WIS in 8 of 9 comparisons across
seasons and horizons (Figure 3A). The 95\% interval for the difference
excluded zero in 7 of 9 comparisons, and the difference was clear in all 3
seasons at 1 week and in 2023--2024 at 2 and 4 weeks.
Averaged over the 3 seasons, the map lowered WIS in every one of the 14
neighborhoods at every horizon, by 6\% to 15\% at 1 week and by 2\% to 8\% at 2
and 4 weeks (Figure 4).

\subsection*{Comparison with tuned machine learning models}

Tuning helped the comparison models more than the graph neural network
(across the 3 cities, validation error fell 7\% for the LSTM and 2\% for the
graph neural network; Appendix S1, Figure S2). The tuned LSTM, which reads all 14 neighborhood series at once without a map,
matched the graph neural network at 1 week in all 3 seasons (mean WIS 5.18
against 5.13; Figure 3B). At 2 and 4 weeks the graph neural
network had lower average error (6.47 vs 6.82 and 8.22 vs 9.16), but the
difference was clear only in 2023--2024. Against tuned Cola-GNN it had lower
average error at every horizon, but no difference was clear after adjustment
(Figure 3C).

\subsection*{Forecast ranges and an example}

Forecast ranges were well calibrated (Figure 5A; Appendix S1, Figure S3):
across horizons, 93\% to 94\% of observed values fell inside the graph neural
network's 95\% intervals and 52\% to 55\% inside its 50\% intervals, similar to
the comparison models. In Dorchester, the neighborhood with the highest rates,
the median forecast rose with the 2024--2025 epidemic earlier than
persistence, which by construction trails the data by \textit{h} weeks, but fell short
of the peak (Figure 5B--D; all neighborhoods in Appendix S1, Figure S4).

\subsection*{Severity alerts}

In Boston, 232 of 2,103 neighborhood-weeks were moderate or higher and 82 were
high or higher. Read as probabilities, the graph neural network's forecasts
warned of moderate weeks better than persistence at 1 and 2 weeks (Brier skill
score 0.61 vs 0.51 and 0.41 vs 0.31; intervals excluded zero) and better than
the no-map version at 1 week (0.61 vs 0.54), similar to the LSTM (Appendix S1,
Figure S5 and Table S12). Read as yes/no alerts from the best guess, they were
no better than persistence for moderate weeks and caught fewer high weeks at
1 week (Peirce skill score 0.40 vs 0.59), because they fell short of the
highest peaks (Figure 6). Their first crossing of a neighborhood's moderate threshold came
a median of 1 week late at 1 week ahead and 2 weeks late at 2 weeks ahead, the
same as persistence (Appendix S1, Table S13). No model warned of high weeks 4 weeks ahead.

\subsection*{Columbus and Chicago}

In Columbus, the graph neural network had the lowest mean WIS of the models in
Figure 2B at every horizon (relative WIS 0.87, 0.85, and 0.75; Appendix S1,
Table S4). In this single season, its leads over persistence and the no-map
version were not clear at any horizon (Appendix S1, Table S7), and at 4 weeks, repeating
the same week of the previous year had lower WIS (5.75 vs 6.31). It warned
of moderate weeks better than persistence on both alert scores at 1 and 2
weeks, but its yes/no alerts caught fewer high weeks, and its first crossings
of the moderate threshold came later (Appendix S1, Tables S12 and S13).

In Chicago, the graph neural network had the lowest mean WIS at every horizon
(relative WIS 0.81, 0.83, and 0.83; Figure 2C; Appendix S1, Table S5), clearly beat persistence in 11 of 12
season-horizon comparisons, and was not clearly worse than any model in any.
Removing the map raised its error in all 12, clearly at 1 week in all 4
seasons and at 2 weeks in 2, but not at 4 weeks (Figure 3A). Unlike in
Boston, it clearly beat the tuned LSTM and Cola-GNN at 1 week in 3 of 4
seasons (Figure 3; Appendix S1, Table S8).

\section*{Discussion}

In Boston, a map of how neighborhoods connect made forecasts more accurate
across 3 seasons, with well-calibrated ranges and lower error in every
neighborhood. The gain over strong alternatives was modest: a tuned LSTM that
sees every neighborhood's series but no map matched it 1 week ahead, and the
graph neural network's lead at 2 and 4 weeks (ahead in 5 of 6 comparisons) was
clear in 1 season of 3. In Columbus, no gain was clear.

Linking areas also gave little at fine scales in New York City and for
dengue in Sri Lanka.\cite{yang2016nyc,gogovi2026spatial} In forecasting hubs,
gains over simple baselines have varied by season and grown at longer
horizons.\cite{mathis2024flusight,cramer2022covid}
One explanation for the small margin over the LSTM is that Boston's
neighborhoods rise and fall largely together. A model that reads all 14 series
at once can learn much of what a neighbor's data would tell it, with or without
a map. If so, the map should matter most to a model that cannot otherwise
see other neighborhoods' series.

Chicago was the strongest test of portability. Designed entirely on Boston,
the model carried over to a city with 4 times as many areas and a different
measure, beating persistence by 17\% to 19\%; its clearer lead over the LSTM
there fits that explanation.

For health departments, the method
needs weekly area-level rates, public boundary files (plus any links across
water), and a season definition. A department with a short surveillance
history can start with a model trained elsewhere, as graph models have
transferred between countries for COVID-19.\cite{panagopoulos2021transfer} In
Columbus, models from other cities matched a local one, and training on 2
cities did better than on 1; Chicago and Boston did better with their own.
For alerts, a department should act on a forecast's probability of crossing
a threshold rather than on its best guess, which falls short of peaks; no model
warned earlier than simply carrying the latest week forward. Before relying on any
forecast, a department should check it against persistence on its own past
seasons. An open-source Python package runs this check and the forecasts on a
department's own data; it reproduced the Boston evaluation within 0.4\% mean
WIS in all 27 model, season, and horizon comparisons.\cite{areaflu2026package}

This study has limitations. Every model, like persistence, ran about
\textit{h} weeks behind the observed curve at \textit{h} weeks ahead; adding a
growth-rate feature or training on median loss did not change this, so the
gains over persistence come from forecasting the size of the epidemic, not
from anticipating its turns. Models trained on past rates largely learn
persistence; earlier warning needs signals that lead ED visits for
epidemiologic reasons, such as wastewater, available within 24 hours of
sampling,\cite{boehm2023wastewater} rather than a larger model. Columbus contributed 1 unseen season, and Chicago's
measure is a capped index rather than a rate, which flattens peaks. All models
used final data, whereas real-time forecasters face reporting delays and
revisions that can make retrospective accuracy look
better.\cite{brooks2018nonmechanistic} Because all inputs
shift together, our 4-week forecasts stand in for 2-week forecasts made with a
2-week reporting delay; we did not examine Boston's data revisions. The
model's structure was fixed on the development season, with only training
settings retuned for each test season; Cola-GNN was
reimplemented from its published description; and Boston's links across water
were added by hand. Finally, because WIS is measured on the scale of the rates,
neighborhoods and weeks with high rates weigh most,\cite{bosse2023transformed}
and ED visits for ILI capture people who seek emergency care, not all
infections.

Next steps are a real-time test in the 2026--2027 season, now under way with
weekly forecasts posted publicly (see Data Availability), ensembles of
models,\cite{reich2019ensemble,sherratt2023european} and wastewater as an
input.

Neighborhood-level influenza forecasts 1 to 4 weeks ahead are feasible from
routine ED surveillance. In a city with a long, complete surveillance history,
a neighborhood map lowers forecast error modestly in every neighborhood, and
its borders can come from public boundary files.

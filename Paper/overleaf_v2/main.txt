\begin{center}
{\Large\bfseries Forecasting Emergency Department Visits for Influenza-Like Illness by
Neighborhood With a Graph Neural Network: Contributions of Spatial,
Environmental, and Demographic Information\par}
\end{center}

\section*{Abstract}

Health departments act by neighborhood, but most influenza forecasts cover
states or regions. We tested which information improves
neighborhood forecasts from a graph neural network given each neighborhood's
recent trajectory, weather, demographic measures, and a map of neighboring
areas. We forecast emergency department visits for
influenza-like illness 1, 2, and 4 weeks ahead in Boston, Massachusetts (14
neighborhoods, 2022--2025); Columbus, Ohio (17 areas, 2024--2025); and Chicago,
Illinois (58 ZIP codes, 2022--2026), on seasons not used in design, with equal tuning,
scored by the weighted interval score. In Boston, the model
had 18\% to 28\% less error than persistence (next week equals this week); a
tuned recurrent neural network matched it 1 week ahead. Removing the map raised
error by 4\% to 11\% in Boston and 4\% to 7\% in Chicago, clearly at 1 week in
every season; removing weather or demographics changed error by under 4\%. Maps
from public boundary files matched a hand-drawn one, and a Boston-trained model
forecast Columbus as well as a local model. Among the inputs to the integrated model, the map of neighboring areas contributed most to neighborhood forecasts.

\bigskip
\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} forecasting; graph neural networks; influenza, human;
machine learning; public health surveillance; small-area analysis

\bigskip
\noindent\textbf{Abbreviations:} ARIMA, autoregressive integrated moving
average; ED, emergency department; ILI, influenza-like illness; LSTM, long
short-term memory network; WIS, weighted interval score.

\section*{Introduction}

In Boston, the average weekly rate of emergency department (ED) visits for
influenza-like illness (ILI) was more than 7 times higher in Dorchester (67 per
100,000 residents) than in Fenway (9 per 100,000) between June 2022 and May
2025.\cite{bphc2026dashboard} Neighborhoods within a single city differ in age
structure, income, and housing, and these differences have long been tied to
who becomes ill with
influenza.\cite{grantz2016chicago,tam2014newhaven,hadler2016poverty,chandrasekhar2017social}
Yet neighborhoods also sit side by side in a densely populated, highly
connected city, where residents commute, work, and shop across
neighborhoods\cite{chang2021mobility,viboud2006synchrony,charu2017mobility}
and where influenza spreads between nearby
places.\cite{gog2014pandemic,pei2018spatial}

Health departments act on a community level. Mobile vaccination clinics,
school outreach, and messaging to clinicians are directed to particular
neighborhoods and
schools,\cite{lambooij2024mobile,benjaminchung2020sliv,shabbat2024detailing}
often before the data that would guide these decisions become
available.\cite{lipsitch2011evidence} Forecasts can help inform these
choices,\cite{lutz2019applying} yet most influenza forecasting targets
nations, regions, and
states.\cite{mcgowan2019collaborative,reich2019collaborative,mathis2024flusight}
Forecasting within a city is harder because each area has fewer cases and
noisier weekly counts,\cite{clayton1987empirical,white2023california} and an
earlier study in New York City found that linking areas improved borough
forecasts but not neighborhood forecasts.\cite{yang2016nyc}

Neighborhood influenza activity is plausibly shaped by several kinds of
information at once: its own recent trajectory and seasonal
timing,\cite{shaman2012forecasting,kandula2019autoregressive} weather
conditions that affect
transmission,\cite{shaman2009humidity,lowen2014humidity,tamerius2013environmental}
the demographic makeup of the neighborhood,\cite{lemaitre2010age,worby2015age,chandrasekhar2017social}
and activity in neighboring areas.\cite{pei2018spatial,yang2016nyc} Forecasting
models have rarely combined them: in a review of 35 validated influenza
forecasting studies, models drew mainly on surveillance series, 6 used
weather, and none reported demographic or neighboring-area
inputs.\cite{chretien2014scoping} Graph neural networks, which pass
information along a map of connections, offer a way to combine all of them in
a single model.\cite{kipf2017gcn,jin2023stgnn,aje2025primer} Graph models have reported gains
for influenza across regions\cite{deng2020colagnn,xie2023epignn,zhu2024gast,luo2025novel}
and for COVID-19 across German districts,\cite{fritz2022combining} but the
evidence is mixed. In forecasting hubs, many models failed to beat simple
baselines in some seasons;\cite{mathis2024flusight,cramer2022covid}
autoregressive models remain competitive 1 to 4 weeks
ahead;\cite{kandula2019autoregressive} and in a study of dengue in Sri Lanka,
a recurrent neural network fitted to each district outperformed every graph
variant.\cite{gogovi2026spatial} Reviews also caution that the added value of
artificial intelligence for epidemics is hard to judge\cite{kraemer2025ai} and
that retrospective tests can overstate real-world
accuracy.\cite{kamyshnyi2026review}

It is therefore unclear which of these kinds of information improve
neighborhood-level forecasts from a graph neural network in a city like
Boston. We addressed this question by forecasting
neighborhood ED visit rates for ILI 1, 2, and 4 weeks ahead in Boston's 14
neighborhoods. We asked 1) whether a graph neural network given all 4 outperforms simple
and machine learning comparison models; 2) which components (environmental,
demographic, and geospatial) lower its error; and 3) whether the map can
be built from public boundary files and whether a model trained in one city
can forecast another. To limit the usual ways a new model can look better than
it is, we tested on seasons never used in design,\cite{tashman2000outofsample}
gave every model the same tuning budget, and scored full forecast
distributions.\cite{gneiting2007proper,bracher2021interval} We repeated the
analysis in Columbus, Ohio, and in Chicago, Illinois, a city never used while
designing the model.

This study makes 4 contributions. First, it evaluates neighborhood-level
influenza forecasts in a way designed to guard against overstated gains.
Second, it separates what the environmental features, the demographic
features, and the neighborhood map each add to a graph neural network, on
seasons not used to design it. Third, it tests whether the map can be built
automatically from public boundary files and whether a model trained in one
city transfers to another, in Boston, Columbus, and Chicago. Fourth, to support
reproducibility, we release the code and frozen settings in a public
repository,\cite{code2026release} along with an open-source Python package
that runs the forecasts and the evaluation on a health department's own
data.\cite{areaflu2026package}

\section*{Methods}

\subsection*{Setting and data}

Table~\ref{tab:data} summarizes the 3 cities: Boston, Columbus, and Chicago. The Boston Public Health
Commission publishes the weekly rate of ED visits for ILI per 100,000
residents for 14 neighborhoods;\cite{bphc2026dashboard} ED visits track
laboratory-confirmed influenza closely.\cite{olson2007ed} Suppressed weeks with small counts were treated as
missing, not zero. Columbus Public Health provided visit-level ED records,
which we aggregated to 17 planning areas and divided by American Community
Survey population.\cite{census2023acs} The Chicago Department of Public Health
publishes a weekly ILI activity level from 1 to 10 for each ZIP
code,\cite{cdph2026influenza} so Chicago errors are in levels (Appendix S1).

Boston and Chicago data are public weekly aggregates; Columbus records were
deidentified and aggregated to weekly area counts before analysis.

\subsection*{Model inputs}

The model received 4 kinds of information for each area and week (Figure 1C).
\textit{Temporal}: the area's rate over the past 16 weeks, a flag for
suppressed weeks, and its usual rate for the time of year (in the baseline
below). \textit{Environmental}: 6
weekly weather variables, including temperature and humidity, from the
Open-Meteo archive,\cite{openmeteo2023} because humidity and temperature shape
influenza transmission.\cite{shaman2009humidity,lowen2014humidity,tamerius2013environmental}
\textit{Demographic}: 8 fixed measures, including poverty, age structure, and
housing, because age and socioeconomic position shape who becomes
ill\cite{lemaitre2010age,hadler2016poverty,chandrasekhar2017social}
(Appendix S1).
\textit{Geospatial}: a map of how areas connect, described next.

\subsection*{Neighborhood map}

Each city is a map in which every area is a point and 2 areas are linked when
they share a border (Figure 1A). The map also includes surrounding areas, never
forecast or scored, so that edge areas have neighbors on every side (7 in Boston, 5 in
Columbus, and 1 in Chicago; Appendix S1).

Boston's map was first drawn by hand. To test whether a new city could skip
that step, we also built it from public boundary
files,\cite{boston2026boundaries,census2023tigerweb,chicago2026zipboundaries}
treating 2 areas as neighbors when their boundaries share an edge rather than
only a corner. Adding links across water by hand is standard in disease
mapping;\cite{morris2019bym} we did so for the Charles River and Boston Harbor.
Besides shared borders, which also reach 2 and 3 borders away at decreasing
weight, the default map links neighborhoods whose past influenza curves were
highly correlated (before the test season only) and neighborhoods with
similar demographic measures (weights in Appendix S1).

\subsection*{Forecasting model}

The graph neural network forecasts each area in 3 steps (Figure 1C). First, a
temporal convolution summarizes the area's rate and suppression flag over the
past 16 weeks, and the current week's weather and the demographic measures
are joined to that summary.
Second, a graph convolution\cite{kipf2017gcn} mixes each area's summary with
its neighbors'. With $\mathbf{H}$ the summaries of all areas stacked as rows,
\begin{equation*}
\mathbf{H}' = \mathrm{LayerNorm}\Bigl(\mathbf{H} + \mathrm{ReLU}\bigl(g_1\,
\tilde{\mathbf{A}} \mathbf{H} \mathbf{W}_1 + g_2\, \mathbf{B} \mathbf{H}
\mathbf{W}_2\bigr)\Bigr),
\end{equation*}
where $\tilde{\mathbf{A}}$ is the normalized table of map links (shared
borders, correlated curves, and similar demographics, combined into one set of
weights), $\mathbf{B}$ is a table of links the model learns for itself,
$\mathbf{W}_1$ and $\mathbf{W}_2$ are learned, and the learned weights $g_1$
and $g_2$ sum to 1. This step is applied twice.
Third, the model adds a learned correction $r_{i,h}$ to a baseline that blends
2 simple forecasts: the current week's rate $y_{i,t}$ and the usual rate for
the target week $c_i(t+h)$, estimated from training seasons with a smooth
seasonal curve,
\begin{equation*}
\hat{y}_{i,t+h} = \pi_h\, y_{i,t} + (1 - \pi_h)\, c_i(t+h) + r_{i,h},
\end{equation*}
with $0 < \pi_h < 1$ learned for each horizon $h$ (weeks ahead; Appendix S1,
Figure S1). Each area's series was scaled by its training-period mean and
standard deviation. A separate model was trained for each horizon, and each
forecast averages 10 training runs.

Each model produced a full forecast distribution, reported as the 23 quantiles
(percentiles) FluSight uses,\cite{mathis2024flusight} with ranges from one
procedure, shared by all models except the FluSight baseline, calibrated on
the weeks just before each test season.

\subsection*{Comparison models}

Four comparison models use only each area's own history: persistence (next
week equals this week); the FluSight baseline, which carries the latest value
forward with ranges from past changes;\cite{mathis2024flusight,cramer2022covid}
the seasonal blend, which is the graph neural network's baseline with no
learned correction ($r_{i,h} = 0$); and a seasonal autoregressive integrated
moving average (ARIMA) model for each area. Three are machine learning models:
gradient-boosted regression trees on the graph neural network's inputs
except the map; a long short-term memory network (LSTM), a recurrent neural network
here reading all areas' series together but with no
map;\cite{hochreiter1997lstm} and Cola-GNN, a published influenza graph neural
network that learns its own connections between locations,\cite{deng2020colagnn}
which we reimplemented. Appendix S1 describes these and 4 further models.

\subsection*{What each component adds}

To measure what each kind of information contributes, we retrained an
otherwise identical model with one removed: 1) no environment, without
the 6 weather variables; 2) no demographics, without the 8 demographic
measures as inputs (links between demographically similar areas belong to the
map and were kept); 3) no map, with no links between areas, mapped or
learned, and no surrounding areas, so each area sees only its own inputs; and
4) none of the three, which sees only each area's own past rates. Each kept
the same baseline. For component $c$, the change in error when it is removed is
\begin{equation*}
\Delta_c = 100 \times \frac{\overline{\mathrm{WIS}}_{-c} -
\overline{\mathrm{WIS}}_{\mathrm{full}}}{\overline{\mathrm{WIS}}_{\mathrm{full}}},
\end{equation*}
where $\overline{\mathrm{WIS}}$ is the mean weighted interval score defined
below; $\Delta_c > 0$ means the component lowered error. If the components are
what separate the graph neural network from history-only methods, the version
with none of them should forecast about as well as those methods. Our
criterion, set before the results were seen, was relative WIS within 5\%
of the seasonal blend's, and no clear difference from it (defined below) in
most season and horizon comparisons.

\subsection*{Evaluation design}

In rolling-origin evaluation,\cite{tashman2000outofsample} every model was
trained only on data before each test season, tuned on the weeks just before
it, and scored on its 52 target weeks (53 in Chicago 2025--2026).

The 2025--2026 season in Boston and Columbus was used to design the model
(the development season; Appendix S1) and is not reported as evidence. The
other seasons were never used in design: 2022--2023, 2023--2024, and 2024--2025 in Boston and
2024--2025 in Columbus. Chicago was added after the design was fixed, so all 4
of its seasons are unseen (Figure 1B).
Influenza nearly disappeared during the first year of the COVID-19
pandemic,\cite{olsen2020decreased} so Columbus models used weeks from July
2022 onward, leaving 1 unseen season; Boston and Chicago models used their
full histories.

Every machine learning model, including each version with a component
removed, got the same tuning budget in every city, test season, and horizon: 12
combinations of settings (its default plus 11 drawn at random), each trained
once, with the best on the validation weeks retrained 10 times (Appendix
S1).

\subsection*{Scoring}

We used the weighted interval score (WIS) to measure forecast accuracy, accounting for both the point forecast and the width and accuracy of its prediction interval. WIS is the scoring rule used by FluSight and the COVID-19 Forecast Hubs.\cite{bracher2021interval,mathis2024flusight} Each forecast gives
a median $m$ and $K = 11$ central intervals $[l_k, u_k]$ (98\% to 10\%); for
observed value $y$,
\begin{equation*}
\mathrm{WIS} = \frac{1}{K + \frac{1}{2}} \Bigl( \tfrac{1}{2}\lvert y - m\rvert
+ \sum_{k=1}^{K} \Bigl[ \tfrac{\alpha_k}{2}(u_k - l_k)
+ (l_k - y)^{+} + (y - u_k)^{+} \Bigr] \Bigr),
\end{equation*}
where $\alpha_k$ is 1 minus the interval's coverage and $(x)^{+} = \max(x,
0)$: interval widths plus penalties for observations outside them (Appendix
S1). It is in the units of the forecast
(ED visits per 100,000, or levels in Chicago), lower is better, and it rewards
ranges that are narrow but still contain what happened. To compare across
cities and horizons, relative WIS is
$\overline{\mathrm{WIS}}_{\mathrm{model}} / \overline{\mathrm{WIS}}_{\mathrm{persistence}}$,
where $\overline{\mathrm{WIS}}$ averages over areas and target weeks within
each season and then over a city's unseen seasons; it is below 1 when the model
beats persistence. Mean absolute error, root mean squared error, and the
correlation between forecast and observed rates score only the best guess, so
they are secondary. We checked calibration by asking how often the observed
value fell inside the 50\% and 95\% forecast intervals.\cite{gneiting2007proper}

To compare any pair of models within a season, we asked whether the gap between them
could be explained by which weeks the season happened to contain. We averaged
WIS over areas for each week, resampled blocks of 4 consecutive weeks for a
95\% confidence interval (2,000 resamples),\cite{kunsch1989bootstrap} and used
the Diebold--Mariano test,\cite{diebold1995comparing} using a small-sample
correction\cite{harvey1997testing} and a variance estimate that allows for the
overlap between consecutive multiweek forecasts.\cite{newey1987hac} Two-sided
\textit{P} values were Holm-adjusted\cite{holm1979simple} within each season
and horizon, separately for the comparison models, the 4 versions with a
component removed, and the history-only check. We call a difference
\textit{clear} when the adjusted \textit{P} value was below .05.

We also scored alerts for moderate and high weeks, with neighborhood
thresholds set by the Moving Epidemic Method\cite{vega2013mem} on earlier
seasons as in the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention's severity
classification,\cite{biggerstaff2018severity} using the Brier skill
score\cite{brier1950verification} for the forecast's probability of crossing a
threshold and hit rate minus false-alarm rate (Peirce skill score) for its best
guess (Appendix S1).

\subsection*{Portability}

With model settings fixed in advance, we reran the Boston evaluation with 2
automatically built maps, for the graph neural network and for a borders-only
version whose map is its only spatial information. We also trained a version
with no neighborhood-specific learned links, which can run in any city, on one
city's weeks before 2024--2025 and applied it to another city's 2024--2025
season as is and after brief further training on local data (fine-tuning):
from Boston to Columbus and Chicago, and from each to Boston. We compared it
with the same model trained from scratch there, and also trained it on 2 or 3
cities at once (Appendix S1).

Analyses used Python 3.12, PyTorch 2.14, PyTorch Geometric 2.8,
statsmodels 0.14, scikit-learn 1.9, and XGBoost 3.4; the code that reproduces
every run is released.\cite{code2026release}

We used generative artificial intelligence (Claude Opus 4.5, 4.6, 4.7, 4.8, 5,
and 5.5; Anthropic)\cite{anthropicopus45,anthropicopus46,anthropicopus47,anthropicopus48,anthropicopus50,anthropicopus55}
through Claude Code to help write and debug code, run the analyses we
specified, find and check references, edit text, and cross-check numbers
against output files. The authors designed the study; chose the data,
comparisons, and evaluation criteria; draft portions of the text; reviewed all code,
outputs, and text; and take full responsibility for the content.

\section*{Results}

\subsection*{How well the full model forecasts}

In Boston, the graph neural network had the lowest mean WIS of the comparison
models at every horizon (Table~\ref{tab:boston}): 5.13 per 100,000 at 1 week, 6.47 at 2
weeks, and 8.22 at 4 weeks, against 6.24, 7.94, and 11.44 for persistence, or
18\% to 28\% less error (Figure 2A). This advantage was clear in all 3 seasons
at 1 week and in 2023--2024 at 2 and 4 weeks (Figure 3A). Point measures largely
agreed (Appendix S1, Table S10).

The margin over tuned machine learning models was smaller, and tuning helped
them more than the graph neural network (across the 3 cities, validation error
fell 7\% for the LSTM and 2\% for the graph neural network; Appendix S1,
Figure S2). The tuned
LSTM, which reads all 14 neighborhood series at once without a map, matched the
graph neural network at 1 week in all 3 seasons (mean WIS 5.18 vs 5.13; Figure
3B). At 2 and 4 weeks the graph neural network had lower error (6.47 vs 6.82
and 8.22 vs 9.16), clearly only in 2023--2024. It also had lower average error
than tuned Cola-GNN at every horizon, but no difference was clear (Figure 3C).

In Chicago, the graph neural network had the lowest mean WIS of the comparison
models at every horizon (relative WIS 0.81, 0.83, and 0.83; Figure 2C), clearly beat persistence in 11
of 12 season-horizon comparisons, and, unlike in Boston, clearly beat the tuned
LSTM and Cola-GNN at 1 week in 3 of 4 seasons (Figure 3). In Columbus, it had
the lowest mean WIS of the models in Figure 2B (relative WIS 0.87, 0.85, and
0.75), but in this single season its lead over persistence, the LSTM, and
Cola-GNN was not clear at any horizon, and at 4 weeks repeating the same week
of the previous year had lower WIS (5.75 vs 6.31; Appendix S1, Tables S4--S9).

Forecast ranges were well calibrated: 93\% to 94\% of observed values fell
inside the graph neural network's 95\% intervals and 52\% to 55\% inside its 50\%
intervals (Figure 4A; Appendix S1, Figure S3). In Dorchester, the median forecast rose with the
2024--2025 epidemic earlier than persistence but fell short of the peak
(Figure 4B--D; Appendix S1, Figure S4). The forecasts' probabilities warned of moderate weeks better
than persistence at 1 and 2 weeks (Brier skill score 0.61 vs 0.51 and 0.41 vs
0.31), but because forecasts fell short of the highest peaks, yes/no alerts
from the best guess caught fewer high weeks at 1 week (Peirce skill score 0.40
vs 0.59) and came no earlier (Appendix S1, Tables S13 and S14, Figures S5 and S6).

\subsection*{What each component adds}

To identify which kinds of information account for the graph neural network’s lead, we removed each in turn. Removing the map raised error the most (Table~\ref{tab:components}; Figure
5C; Appendix S1, Table S15). In Boston, error rose by 10.8\% at 1
week, 3.8\% at 2 weeks, and 5.6\% at 4 weeks, and the increase was clear in all
3 seasons at 1 week and in 2 of 3 at 2 and 4 weeks. Averaged over the 3
seasons, the map lowered WIS in every one of the 14 neighborhoods at every
horizon, by 6\% to 15\% at 1 week and by 2\% to 8\% at 2 and 4 weeks (Figure
6). In Chicago, removing the map raised error by 7.4\%, 6.0\%, and 3.6\%,
clearly at 1 week in all 4 seasons and at 2 weeks in 3, but not clearly at 4
weeks. In Columbus's single season, error rose by 15.8\% at 1 week, not
clearly.

Removing the environmental features or the demographic features did not consistently change forecast error (Figure 5A and B). In Boston, error changed by $-$0.9\% to $+$2.1\%, and 1 of 18 week-level
comparisons was clear (demographics, 2022--2023, 1 week). In Chicago, the
version without demographics never differed clearly from the full model, and
the version without weather was clearly better in 3 of 12 comparisons and
clearly worse in 1. In Columbus, neither differed clearly.

Removing all 3 at once, so that the model saw only each area's own past
rates, cost about as much as removing the map alone; relative WIS was within
0.01 of the no-map version's in every city and horizon (Figure 5D).

The past-rates-only version forecast better than the comparison models that
use only an area's own history, failing the criterion we set in advance:
its relative WIS was 1\% to 9\% below the seasonal blend's (within 5\%
in 4 of 9 city and horizon pairs), and it was clearly better than the seasonal
blend in 12 of 24 season and horizon comparisons, never clearly worse
(Appendix S1, Table S16). It also had less error than seasonal ARIMA in Boston
and Columbus and was within 3\% of it in Chicago, while the tuned LSTM had less error than it at 1 week in Boston
and Chicago. Learning from past rates adds a little; the map adds most of
the rest. On the
development season, weather had appeared to help slightly, which the unseen
seasons did not confirm (Appendix S1, Table S17).

\subsection*{Transferability}

Automatically built maps performed as well as the hand-drawn Boston map. It had 18
or 19 of its 23 borders right compared with the 2 automatically built maps, and
replacing it made no clear difference in any of 36 week-level comparisons,
including for a model whose only spatial information is the map
(Table~\ref{tab:maps}).

Transfer between cities worked in Columbus but not consistently elsewhere. A Boston-trained model
applied to Columbus with no local training had a mean WIS of 3.90, 5.08, and
6.45 at 1, 2, and 4 weeks, against 4.11, 5.18, and 6.49 for the same model
trained in Columbus, with no clear difference (Table~\ref{tab:transfer}). In
Chicago, with nearly 5 years of its own history, it was clearly worse than a
local model at 1 and 2 weeks. Models moved to Boston were worse than a
Boston-trained model, clearly at 4 weeks (10.07 and 10.36 vs 8.25). Every
transferred model beat persistence, fine-tuning never clearly differed from
training from scratch, and training on several cities at once never clearly
beat a local model (Appendix S1, Table S18).

\section*{Discussion}

On seasons never used to design it, a graph neural network given each
neighborhood's recent trajectory, weather, demographic measures, and a map of
neighboring areas forecast ED visits for ILI with 18\% to 28\% less error than
persistence in Boston and 17\% to 19\% less in Chicago.
Its gain over a model given only past rates was attributable to the map; removing the environmental or demographic features changed error by $-$0.9\% to $+$2.1\% in Boston, so in these data they added no consistent benefit. The margin over strong alternatives was modest: a tuned LSTM with no map
matched the graph neural network 1 week
ahead in Boston, and in Columbus, with 1 unseen season, no gain was clear.

One explanation for why the map mattered when the environmental and demographic features did not is how the inputs were prepared. Each area's
series was scaled by its own mean, so fixed differences between neighborhoods,
the kind the demographic measures describe, were absorbed before the model saw
them. Neighborhoods in one city share nearly the same weather, and its seasonal
part is already carried by the baseline; humidity and temperature shape when
seasons occur,\cite{shaman2009humidity,lowen2014humidity} but given recent rates and the time of year, they may have had little additional information to offer. The map, by
contrast, tells the model what is happening now in nearby areas, from which
influenza tends to arrive.\cite{gog2014pandemic,pei2018spatial} That the
LSTM also beat the version with past rates only suggests the gain comes from
other areas' series more than from the particular map.

Linking areas gave little for New York City neighborhoods and for dengue in
Sri Lankan districts,\cite{yang2016nyc,gogovi2026spatial} whereas here the map's gain was
clear at the neighborhood scale in 2 of 3 cities, including Chicago, which had
4 times as many areas and played no part in the design. As in forecasting hubs, where gains over simple baselines have varied
over time,\cite{mathis2024flusight,cramer2022covid} the advantage over the
tuned LSTM varied, small in Boston but clear at 1 week in 3 of 4 Chicago
seasons.

For health departments, the method needs weekly area-level rates, public
boundary files, and a season definition; weather and demographic data can be
left out. A department with a short history can start with a model trained
elsewhere, as graph models have transferred between countries for
COVID-19,\cite{panagopoulos2021transfer} though Boston and Chicago did better
with their own. For alerts, a forecast's probability of crossing a threshold
is more useful than its best guess, which falls short of peaks. An
open-source Python package runs the forecasts and a check against persistence
on a department's own data; once trained, its weekly forecasts take about a
minute on a laptop (Appendix S1, Table S19). With this study's model it reproduced the
Boston evaluation within 0.4\% mean WIS;\cite{areaflu2026package} by default
it leaves out the demographic measures and links.

This study has limitations. Components were removed one at a time, so weather
and demographic measures might matter together or in cities with more varied
climate.
Every model, like persistence, ran about \textit{h} weeks behind the observed
curve at \textit{h} weeks ahead, so the gains come from forecasting the size of
the epidemic, not its turns; earlier warning likely needs leading signals such
as wastewater, available within 24 hours of sampling.\cite{boehm2023wastewater}
Columbus contributed 1 unseen season, and Chicago's measure is a capped index
that flattens peaks. All models used final data, not the delayed and revised
data that real-time forecasters face, which can make retrospective accuracy
look better.\cite{brooks2018nonmechanistic} Cola-GNN was reimplemented from
its description, and Boston's links across water were added by hand. Because
WIS is on the scale of the rates, high-rate neighborhoods and weeks weigh
most,\cite{bosse2023transformed} and ED visits for ILI capture people who seek
emergency care, not all infections.

Next steps are the real-time test under way in 2026--2027 (see Data
Availability), ensembles of
models,\cite{reich2019ensemble,sherratt2023european} and new inputs and
places. Boston's influenza wastewater series began in July 2024, too late to
train any unseen season's model (Appendix S1). Neighborhood vaccination
coverage, held by health departments but not public, may help where fixed
demographics did not. Cities abroad, such as Buenos Aires, Argentina, would
test how far the map's gain generalizes.

Neighborhood-level influenza forecasts 1 to 4 weeks ahead are feasible from
routine ED surveillance. Of the components added to each area's own history,
only the map linking neighboring areas clearly lowered error. It can be built
from public boundary files, while weather and demographic measures added
little.

\noindent\textbf{Figure 1.} Study design and model for neighborhood-level
influenza forecasting in Boston, Massachusetts; Columbus, Ohio; and Chicago,
Illinois, 2017--2026. A) Boston's 14 neighborhoods, shaded by mean weekly rate
of emergency department visits for influenza-like illness per 100,000
residents, June 2022--May 2025. Solid lines link neighborhoods that share a
border in the official land boundary file; dashed lines are links across the
Charles River and Boston Harbor, which were added by hand. Other surrounding areas are not shown. The
evaluated model used the hand-drawn map, which differs from this one in 5 of
its 23 borders. B) Data used in each city and the test seasons. Each model
was trained only on data before a test season and scored on its target weeks.
The development season, used to design the model, is not reported as evidence;
Chicago, added after the design was fixed, has none. C) The graph neural
network. Each area's past 16 weeks of rates enter a temporal convolution that
looks only at past weeks; the current week's weather and the demographic
measures are joined to its summary; and the neighborhood map enters at the
graph convolution, which mixes each area's summary with its neighbors'. The model's learned correction is added to a baseline that blends
the current week with the seasonal curve, and the output is 23 quantiles of the
forecast distribution. Italic notes give the version of the model in which each
input is removed (Figure 5).

\noindent\textit{Alt text:} Top left, a map of Boston's 14 neighborhoods
shaded from light to dark blue, darkest in Dorchester and Roxbury, with dots at
each neighborhood connected by lines to bordering neighborhoods and dashed
lines to 2 water points. Top right, horizontal bars for Boston, Columbus, and
Chicago showing years of available data, blue blocks for unseen test seasons
(3 for Boston, 1 for Columbus, 4 for Chicago), and gray blocks for the Boston
and Columbus development seasons. Bottom, a flow diagram: 4 input boxes
(temporal, environmental, demographic, geospatial) on the left; the temporal
box points to a temporal convolution box, the environmental and demographic
boxes join its output, and the geospatial box points to a graph convolution
box; the graph convolution and a baseline box both point to a plus
sign, which points to an output box of 23 quantiles 1, 2, and 4 weeks ahead.

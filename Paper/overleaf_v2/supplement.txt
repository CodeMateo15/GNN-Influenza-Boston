\section*{How the map is built}

The map is a table of link weights $A_{ij}$ between areas $i$ and $j$. It is
the sum of 4 parts, and a pair linked in more than one way gets the weights
added:
\begin{equation*}
A_{ij} = \mathbb{1}[\text{$i$ and $j$ share a border}]
 + 0.5^{\,d_{ij}}\,\mathbb{1}[2 \le d_{ij} \le 3]
 + r_{ij}\,\mathbb{1}[r_{ij} > 0.85]
 + s_{ij}\,\mathbb{1}[s_{ij} > 0.5].
\end{equation*}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Shared borders} have weight 1. Boston has 23 borders between
its 14 neighborhoods and 21 more to its 7 surrounding areas:
Cambridge/Somerville, the Charles River, Chelsea/Revere/Winthrop, Boston
Harbor, the southern suburbs (Milton and Quincy), the western suburbs (Newton
and Dedham), and Brookline. Columbus has 39 borders between its 17 planning
areas and 9 to its 5 surrounding fringe ZIP codes (see Data notes by city).
Chicago has 113 borders between its 58 scored ZIP codes and 2 to ZIP code
60827, which is in the map but not scored. Surrounding areas have no
influenza series and are never forecast or scored.
\item \textbf{Areas 2 or 3 borders apart}, where $d_{ij}$ counts borders
between scored areas only, have weight 0.25 and 0.125: 29 and 22 pairs in
Boston, 58 and 35 in Columbus, and 198 and 236 in Chicago.
\item \textbf{Correlated curves.} $r_{ij}$ is the Pearson correlation of the
2 areas' weekly rates over the training weeks before each test season, so it
uses no test data. Pairs above 0.85 are linked with weight $r_{ij}$: 30, 27,
and 20 of 91 pairs in Boston for the 2022--2023, 2023--2024, and 2024--2025
seasons; 7 in Columbus; and 61, 2, 0, and 0 in Chicago for 2022--2023 to
2025--2026. Chicago's capped activity levels correlate less closely as more
seasons enter the training weeks.
\item \textbf{Similar demographic measures.} Each of the 8 measures is
rescaled to run from 0 to 1 across a city's areas, and
$s_{ij} = \exp\bigl(-\lVert z_i - z_j \rVert^2 / 2\sigma^2\bigr)$, where
$z_i$ holds area $i$'s 8 rescaled measures and $\sigma$ is the median
distance between pairs of areas. Pairs above 0.5 are linked with weight
$s_{ij}$: 60 of 91 pairs in Boston, 85 of 136 in Columbus, and 1,107 of 1,653
in Chicago.
\end{itemize}
Every area is also linked to itself with weight 1, and the table is
normalized as is usual for a graph convolution,\cite{kipf2017gcn}
$\tilde{\mathbf{A}} = \mathbf{D}^{-1/2}(\mathbf{A} + \mathbf{I})\mathbf{D}^{-1/2}$,
where $\mathbf{D}$ holds the row sums of $\mathbf{A} + \mathbf{I}$. The
learned links $\mathbf{B}$ in the main-text equation are
$\mathrm{softmax}\bigl(\mathrm{ReLU}(\mathbf{E}_1\mathbf{E}_2^{\top})\bigr)$,
taken row by row, where $\mathbf{E}_1$ and $\mathbf{E}_2$ give each area 2
learned vectors of length 8. The version with the map removed drops
$\tilde{\mathbf{A}}$, $\mathbf{B}$, and the surrounding areas. On the
development season, removing the correlation or demographic links changed
accuracy far less than removing all links (Table~\ref{tab:ablation}).

\section*{Comparison models: ARIMA and LSTM}

\textbf{Seasonal ARIMA.} Each area has its own model, fitted to its weekly
rates with statsmodels. The annual cycle enters as 3 pairs of sine and cosine
terms of the day of the year, used as regressors (dynamic harmonic
regression), because a lag-52 seasonal term needs more full seasons than the
data hold. The regressors depend on the calendar only, so their future values
are known when the forecast is made. For each area, the order $(p, d, q)$,
with $p$ and $q$ from 0 to 3 and $d$ 0 or 1, was chosen by root mean squared
error of 1-week forecasts on the validation weeks before each test season.
The model was then fitted once on the training weeks and updated with each
new observed week without refitting. A forecast more than 10 times an area's
highest past rate, or a failed fit, was replaced by the last observed value;
this happened in at most 0.4\% of forecasts in any city, season, and horizon. Suppressed weeks are left
missing, which the state-space fit handles directly.

\textbf{LSTM.} One network reads all of a city's areas together: each week
is a vector of every area's rate, so the network can use other areas' series
but has no map. A single LSTM layer\cite{hochreiter1997lstm} reads the past weeks
(by default 8 weeks and 64 hidden units), and a linear layer gives every area's
forecast for one horizon. Rates are scaled per area by the mean and standard
deviation of the training weeks. Suppressed weeks are filled from earlier
weeks in the inputs and skipped in the loss, a mean squared error. Training
uses Adam and stops when validation error has not improved for 25 epochs. As
an option in tuning, the network can predict a correction to the same
seasonal blend the graph neural network uses instead of the rate itself.
Hidden size, window, target, learning rate, weight decay, dropout, and epochs
were tuned as in Table~\ref{tab:space}, and forecasts are the mean of 10
training runs. Both models received forecast intervals from the same procedure
as the other models.

\section*{The weighted interval score}

The weighted interval score (WIS) asks how far off a forecast was, counting
both its best guess and how honest its range was.\cite{bracher2021interval} A
close forecast that claims too much certainty should score worse than an
equally close one that admits its uncertainty, and a very wide range should
not score well only because it happened to contain the observed value.

The WIS is built from the interval score. For a central $(1-\alpha)\times100\%$ forecast interval
from $l$ to $u$ and an observed value $y$, the interval score is
\begin{equation*}
\mathrm{IS}_\alpha(l, u; y) = (u - l)
+ \frac{2}{\alpha}\,(l - y)\,\mathbf{1}(y < l)
+ \frac{2}{\alpha}\,(y - u)\,\mathbf{1}(y > u),
\end{equation*}
where $\mathbf{1}(\cdot)$ is 1 when the condition holds and 0 otherwise. The
first term is the width of the interval; the other 2 are penalties for missing,
proportional to how far outside the interval the observation fell. For a 95\%
interval ($\alpha = 0.05$), each unit of miss costs 40 times as much as each
unit of width, so the score is lowest, on average, for an interval that
really does contain the outcome 95\% of the time.\cite{gneiting2007proper}

Every model in this study reports the 23
quantiles used by FluSight: the median ($m$) and 11 central intervals with
$\alpha_k = 0.02, 0.05, 0.10, 0.20, \ldots, 0.90$ (98\%, 95\%, 90\%, 80\%,
\ldots, 10\% intervals). The WIS combines them:
\begin{equation*}
\mathrm{WIS}(y) = \frac{1}{K + \tfrac{1}{2}}
\left( \tfrac{1}{2}\,\lvert y - m \rvert
+ \sum_{k=1}^{K} \frac{\alpha_k}{2}\,\mathrm{IS}_{\alpha_k}(l_k, u_k; y) \right),
\qquad K = 11.
\end{equation*}
Each interval is weighted by $\alpha_k/2$, so narrow intervals near the median
count with small weight and wide intervals with larger weight, and the median
alone contributes half its absolute error. The WIS is in the units of the
forecast (ED visits for ILI per 100,000, or Chicago's 1--10 levels), and lower
is better. A forecast that puts all its quantiles on a single value has a WIS
equal to its absolute error, so WIS can be read on the same scale as mean
absolute error. The WIS approximates the continuous ranked probability score,
a standard score for whole forecast distributions, and is algebraically equal
to twice the mean quantile (pinball) loss over the 23 quantiles, which is how
the code computes it. Each table reports the mean WIS over neighborhoods and
target weeks; relative WIS divides a model's mean WIS by that of persistence.

The WIS can be split exactly into a spread term (the weighted widths,
$\sum_k \tfrac{\alpha_k}{2}(u_k - l_k)$, divided by $K + \tfrac{1}{2}$) and
penalties for forecasting too high or too low (the weighted misses, including
the median term). Most of a wide, cautious forecast's score comes from spread,
and most of a narrow forecast's score comes from penalties when it misses.

In Boston, the graph neural network's mean WIS 1 week ahead was 5.13 (Table 2
of the main text): its forecasts were off by about 5 ED visits for ILI per
100,000 residents per neighborhood-week, with credit given for honest ranges. For scale, observed rates in the 3 unseen
seasons averaged 26.9 per 100,000 (median, 15.5; 90th percentile, 60.9). The
WIS is lower than the same forecasts' mean absolute error (7.85; Table~\ref{tab:pointboston})
because a forecast whose range covers the outcome is charged less than one
that commits to a single value. Of the 5.13, about 1.9 came from the width of
the ranges, 2.1 from forecasting too low, and 1.2 from forecasting too high.
The mean is driven by high weeks. In the tenth of neighborhood-weeks with the
highest rates, the average WIS was 16.2, against 3.9 in the rest, and those
weeks supplied about a third of the total; a typical (median) week scored 2.8.
WIS values cannot be compared across cities with different measures: Boston's
(about 5 to 8) are in rates and Chicago's (about 1 to 1.5) in 1--10 levels.
Within a city they also rise and fall with how high a season ran, so models
are compared with each other on the same weeks. For this reason the main text
leads with relative WIS. Persistence's mean WIS 1 week ahead was 6.24, so the
graph neural network's relative WIS was 5.13/6.24 = 0.82, or 18\% less error
than persistence.

As an example, consider Dorchester. The graph neural network's forecast
made on January 19, 2025, for the week of February 2 (2 weeks ahead) had a
median of 226.7 per 100,000 and a 95\% interval of 175.6 to 292.6. The
observed rate was 294.4, just above the 97.5th percentile. The WIS for this
forecast was 43.5, of which 6.1 came from spread and 37.4 from
underprediction. The model forecast a rise but too low a peak, and nearly all
of the score reflects that shortfall. Persistence, which carried forward the
292.5 observed on January 19, scored 9.0 that week. Forecasts that fall short
of peaks, as described in the main text, are penalized most because errors
are largest where rates are high.\cite{bosse2023transformed}

\section*{Data notes by city}

\textbf{Inputs shared by all cities.} The 6 weekly weather variables are mean,
maximum, and minimum temperature, relative humidity, precipitation, and maximum
wind speed, from the Open-Meteo archive. The 8 demographic measures in all 3 cities
are population density, poverty, share of children, share aged 65
years or older, homeownership, transit commuting, households without a vehicle,
and share of nonwhite residents, from city planning tables in Boston and from
American Community Survey 5-year estimates in Columbus and Chicago. Each
measure is scaled within its own city.

\textbf{Boston.} Rates are published weekly by the Boston Public Health
Commission for 14 neighborhoods (Back Bay, Beacon Hill, Downtown, North End, and
West End form a single neighborhood). Suppressed weeks were treated as missing
and excluded from training and scoring. Two data-loading errors found during the
project (a count row averaged with the rate row, and suppressed weeks read as
zero) were corrected before any result reported here was produced.

\textbf{Columbus.} Visit-level ED records for influenza-like illness from
Columbus Public Health were assigned to 17 planning areas through the
department's ZIP code crosswalk. Population denominators come from the American
Community Survey 5-year estimates summed over ZIP code tabulation areas. Areas
with no visits in a week are recorded as observed zeros. The 5 surrounding
areas in the map are fringe ZIP codes (43002, 43035, 43140, 43146, and 43240)
that the crosswalk lists but leaves unassigned to any planning area; they carry
0.2\% of visits.

\textbf{Chicago.} The Chicago Department of Public Health publishes a weekly
influenza-like illness activity level for each ZIP code on the City of Chicago
Data Portal (dataset 8vvr-jv2g), compiled from Illinois emergency department
syndromic surveillance. The level compares a ZIP code's share of emergency
department visits for influenza-like illness with that ZIP code's own summer
average: level 1 is at or below it, each level above is roughly 1 more
standard deviation, and the scale stops at 10, which 13\% of ZIP-weeks reach.
Because it is an index with no denominator, errors are in levels and cannot be
compared in size with the other cities. The series begins in September 2019;
the city is absent for May--September 2020, and those weeks are missing. Of 60
ZIP codes, 1 (60106, mostly outside the city) has no boundary and is dropped,
and 1 (60827) that joined the feed in September 2020 is in the map but not
scored, leaving 58. O'Hare (60666) has no residents and takes the city median
for its demographic measures. The map has 115 shared borders, derived from the
city's ZIP code boundary file. Models were trained on all weeks from September
2019, and every Chicago season from 2022--2023 to 2025--2026 is unseen. One ZIP
code (60707) reported level 1 in every week before June 2022, so in the
2022--2023 training data its series had no variation. Each series is scaled by
its training-period standard deviation; a series with none is scaled by 1
instead, which leaves every other series and city unchanged. An earlier run
that divided by a near-zero value gave that ZIP code test inputs near 10$^{9}$,
which graph models passed to its neighbors; all 2022--2023 Chicago results
reported here are from the corrected runs.

\section*{Severity alerts}

\textbf{Thresholds.} For each neighborhood (or Columbus area), intensity
thresholds come from the Moving Epidemic Method,\cite{vega2013mem} reimplemented in
Python following the defaults of the \texttt{mem} R package, at the 50th, 90th and 98th percentile levels CDC uses.
Each test season uses only earlier, non-pandemic seasons, and data stop the day
the test season starts: Boston 2022--2023 uses 2017--2018 and 2018--2019;
2023--2024 adds 2022--2023; 2024--2025 adds 2023--2024. Columbus uses 2022--2023
and 2023--2024. The 2019--2020 through 2021--2022 seasons are left out because
COVID-19 truncated or suppressed them. Chicago is not included: its measure is
already the health department's graded 1--10 activity level.

\textbf{Scores.} A week is an event when the observed rate is at or above the
threshold. The probability score uses each forecast's probability of being at
or above the threshold, read from its calibrated 95\% interval as a normal
distribution; the Brier skill score compares its mean squared error with
always forecasting the pooled share of event weeks. The yes/no score alerts
when the best-guess forecast is at or above the threshold; the Peirce skill
score is the share of event weeks alerted minus the share of non-event weeks
alerted. Both pool all neighborhood-weeks of a city's unseen seasons.
Intervals for differences use the moving-block bootstrap of the main text.
The code is in the released repository (blinded for review).

\section*{What each component adds on the unseen seasons}

Each version of the graph neural network with one component removed (main
text, Methods) was tuned with the same budget as the full model and averaged
over 10 training runs; the task lists and commands are in the released code
(repository blinded for review). Table~\ref{tab:components}
compares each version with the full model on the weeks of one season. ``More
error'' is the version's mean WIS minus the full model's, as a percentage of the
full model's, so a positive value means the component lowered error; intervals
are 95\% confidence intervals from a moving-block bootstrap over weeks (blocks
of 4 weeks) on the same scale. \textit{P} values are from the 2-sided
Diebold--Mariano test with the Harvey--Leybourne--Newbold correction,
Holm-adjusted across the 4 versions within each season and horizon.

Table~\ref{tab:historyonly} asks whether the version given only past rates
forecasts like the comparison models that use only an area's own history.
Here the reference is that version, and a positive value means the comparison
model had more error. The 5 comparisons in each season and horizon form their
own Holm family. Our criterion, set before the results were seen, was relative
WIS within 5\% of the seasonal blend's and no clear difference from it in most
comparisons. Neither part was met: relative WIS was 5.2\%, 7.1\%, and 9.0\%
lower than the seasonal blend's at 1, 2, and 4 weeks in Boston, 6.1\%, 4.9\%,
and 1.4\% lower in Columbus, and 5.7\%, 4.8\%, and 2.9\% lower in Chicago; the
difference was clear in 12 of 24 season and horizon comparisons, each time
favoring the version with past rates only.

\textbf{Data version.} The Boston Public Health Commission later revised 5
weekly neighborhood values before June 2025 (3 in the 2022--2023 test
season). Every Boston model compared in the main text, including these
versions, used the file as it stood before the revision, so that all were
scored against the same observations. The multicity models in
Table~\ref{tab:pooled} were trained after the revision; all 5 revised values
fall in their training weeks, none in the 2024--2025 season on which they were
scored. The released code documents both versions.

\section*{Design experience on the development season}

Before the test seasons were scored, we chose the model's structure on the
development season (2025--2026 in Boston; models trained on weeks from July
2022). To decide what to keep, we changed one part of the model at a time,
retrained it from scratch with 10 training runs, and asked whether the change
made its forecasts rise and fall more closely with what happened in each
neighborhood. The measure is the correlation between forecast and observed
rates, averaged over the 14 neighborhoods; Table~\ref{tab:ablation} gives each
change minus the default, so a positive value means the changed model tracked
the observed curve better. To judge how large a difference has to be before it
means anything, we trained the default 3 times on different random starting
points. Those 3 results differed by up to 0.003, 0.006, and 0.031 at 1, 2, and 4
weeks, so smaller differences are within run-to-run noise. The table also
counts the neighborhoods in which the change raised the correlation. These
results were used only to make design choices. They come from the season used
to design the model and are not evidence of performance, which comes from the
unseen seasons in the main text, where removing the map, weather, the
demographic measures, or all 3 is tested week by week (Figure 5;
Table~\ref{tab:components}). There, the map's effect was confirmed, the
demographic measures again added nothing, and the small gain from weather seen
here was not.

Removing all links between neighborhoods lowered the correlation by 0.075 at 1
week and 0.093 at 2 weeks, in every neighborhood, but made no difference beyond
noise at 4 weeks. Which links were used mattered much less. Keeping borders only,
or removing the correlation or demographic links, changed the correlation by
0.006 or less at 1 and 2 weeks, and so did keeping only the links the model
learns for itself. Removing weather lowered the correlation at every horizon
and in every neighborhood.
The 8 demographic measures did not help as inputs: removing them changed the
correlation by 0.003 or less at 1 and 2 weeks and raised it by 0.027 at 4 weeks,
in all 14 neighborhoods. They remained in the evaluated model. The blended baseline mattered most at 4 weeks, where
replacing it with the current week alone lowered the correlation by 0.096.

Of the inputs we tried adding, Boston's influenza wastewater series raised the
correlation by 0.089 at 4 weeks, in all 14 neighborhoods, but lowered it by
0.017 at 1 week. The series begins in July 2024 and covers under half of the
training period, so the 4-week gain is uncertain, and wastewater was not part of
the evaluated model. It also begins after the training period of every unseen
Boston season (the latest ends in May 2024), so it could not be tested there. Statewide vaccination uptake also helped at 4 weeks
(0.041). Neighborhood COVID-19 and RSV rates with COVID-19 wastewater made
forecasts worse at every horizon. Links weighted by public transit ridership,
measured in a separate study, changed the correlation by $-0.001$, $-0.007$, and
$+0.015$; nearly every transit link duplicated an existing link. Finally,
in an earlier, untuned run of our reimplementation of the dual-topology model of
Luo et al.,\cite{luo2025novel} adding a node for the surrounding area raised the
correlation from 0.412 to 0.417, compared with the 0.135 gain reported in the
original study.

\section*{Training on several cities at once}

Because Columbus is forecast about as well by a Boston-trained model as by its
own, we asked whether training on more than 1 city helps further. We used the
same model and settings as in Table 5 of the main text (no
neighborhood-specific learned links, settings fixed in advance, 10 training
runs of 120 passes) and trained it on several cities together, using only weeks
before the 2024--2025 test season in each. Each city kept its own map, its own
scaling (each area's series standardized by its own training-period mean and
standard deviation), and its own validation weeks. Every training batch came
from 1 city, each city supplied the same number of batches per pass so that
Chicago's 58 areas did not outweigh Boston's 14 or Columbus's 17, and the stopping
point was chosen on the average of the 3 cities' validation errors. We trained
2 versions: one on all 3 cities, which includes the target city's own earlier
weeks, and one on the other 2 cities only, which, like the transferred models
of Table 5, has seen no data from the target. Each was applied to each city's
2024--2025 season as is and after fine-tuning on local data, and compared week
by week with the model trained on the target city alone (Holm adjustment over
the 4 multicity versions at each horizon).

No multicity model was clearly more accurate than the locally trained one
(Table~\ref{tab:pooled}). In Columbus, every multicity version had lower mean
WIS than the local model at every horizon, by 3\% to 8\%, but none of these
differences was clear (adjusted \textit{P} values .06 to .40). The model
trained on Boston and Chicago, with no Columbus data, beat the Boston-only model of Table 5 at 1, 2, and 4
weeks (3.85 vs 3.90, 4.80 vs 5.08, and 5.97 vs 6.45), clearly at 2 weeks
(\textit{P} = .006) and nearly so at 4 weeks (\textit{P} = .06). In Boston and
Chicago, which have long histories, their own data were best. Without further
training, the multicity models were worse than the local ones at every
horizon in both cities, clearly in 3 of the 4 Chicago comparisons at 1 and 2
weeks; after fine-tuning they tied the local models in all 12 comparisons.
Training on 2 cities instead of 1 did not help Boston (adjusted \textit{P} >
.99 against each single-city source) and helped Chicago only slightly, clearly
at 1 week (1.14 vs 1.16, \textit{P} = .04) but not at 2 or 4 weeks.

\section*{Computing time and energy}

Table~\ref{tab:compute} gives the time and electricity for one evaluation run
of each model: the final settings trained and used to forecast the 52 target
weeks of Boston's 2024--2025 season, at each horizon. For learned models this
covers all 10 training runs; tuning (12 single training runs of different
settings) is not included. Every run used 1 processor core of Northeastern
University's Explorer cluster (Intel Xeon E5-2680 v4) and no graphics card.
Energy was estimated with CodeCarbon 3.2.5 from processor load and rated power,
not read from a power meter, with processor energy scaled to the single core
allocated; it is suited to ranking models on the same hardware, not to exact
totals. At the Massachusetts grid's carbon intensity (about 372 g of carbon
dioxide per kWh), the graph neural network's 17 to 24 Wh per run is 6 to 9 g
of carbon dioxide, and the tuned LSTM's under 0.1 Wh is under 0.04 g.

The graph neural network took about 1 hour per run, 100 to 280 times as long
as the tuned LSTM and 30 to 45 times as long as Cola-GNN. Almost all of that time is
training. In weekly use, a model trained once at the start of the season and
loaded each week, as in this evaluation, forecasts all 4 horizons for Boston in
about 80 seconds on a laptop (Apple M5).
